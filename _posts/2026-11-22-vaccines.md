My poor wife is stuck in bed with what I presume is the flu. She's not one for staying in one place for long, but she has been confined to our bedroom for almost 48 hours now. She slept for around thirteen hours last night, woke up around lunch (which I've never seen her do), and is now napping again. I know she must feel really awful.

Of course, I'm doing what I can to take care of her. I keep bringing her food, water, and medicine whenever she needs it.

I hate that she feels so bad, but selfishly I'm thankful that so far I have managed to avoid the illness. Two nights ago I had a very minor cough and the tiniest bit of sinus pressure, but on the whole I think I managed to avoid it, a blessing I fully credit to getting my yearly preventative jab.

A little over a week ago I received my yearly flu and covid vaccines. When you are a transplant patient with a weakened immune system, you are given a higher strength dose than what is usually given to a relatively healthy person. Normally these doses are reserved for people 65 and older, but immunocompromised people are elligible for it as well. You just have to get them from your doctor, rather than at the pharmacy. That's at least how it is in the United States, your mileage may vary in your country.

The higher dose is necessary because the "regular strength" dose just isn't nearly as effective when your immune system is weakened. You have to crank up the volume to have a chance at building up enough antibodies to be effective against the real thing.

It's funny, because I go to a "teaching clinic" to see my general practitioner, and when the nurse came in after the doctor entered the orders she had a med student following her around like a shadow. "Now why are we giving you such a strong dose?" She asked, and I replied that I had received a new kidney. She turned around to the med student and said, "ah, some people who have had transplants get these higher doses. If either one of us took this we'd be knocked out through the weekend! But he probably won't even notice." How reassuring! After that, I had to sign a form giving them permission to pump me full of the good stuff, and a couple pricks in the arm later, it was done.

I had a sore arm after, and maybe I felt a little tired, but on the whole I never really experienced any symptoms of the vaccines. And now, a week later, I am beyond thankful that the timing of those vaccines worked out so that it could shield me from such direct exposure.

I probably don't need to tell readers of this blog, but vaccines are so incredibly important. They help protect you, and they help protect the people you come in contact with (especially the very young, the very old, and the people like me with very weakened immune symptoms). The vaccines are super quick, usually painless, and are almost always free. 

Please, get your vaccines. They're an absolute modern miracle.
