I've been writing in various forms since I was a kid. In middle school I was dead-set on becoming a professional writer as an adult, presumably churning out book after book to eek out a living. By high school, though, that ember had all but snuffed itself out. By then I had fallen in love with computers, and swiftly discovered Linux and open source, and my future took a completely different path.

But even though my focus is largely on technology, I still immensely enjoy writing, and find myself often doing it in both personal (mostly blog posts) and professional (project documentation, release announcements, and support queries) capacities.

The area I've struggled with most is brevity. Although I'm a bit quiet in day-to-day conversation, when I sit down and start writing I tend to lay it on pretty thick. That's not necessarily a bad thing, but it comes at the expense of clarity. The more time goes on, the more I value efficiency and being unambiguous. 

In that spirit, I've been actively trying to improve my writing. I've learned to be a better writer through osmosis in working with my colleagues, and especially in having many of the things I write be subjected not only to edits by other people on the team, but also a brilliant (and ruthless!) professional editorial team.

Here are the things I've learned to make my writing better:

* Eliminate the word "just." I used to throw out the word "just" for things constantly, but it always makes for a weaker sentence. I no longer say "I just wanted to tell you," or "I just wanted to send this email because." I get on with it, straight away, no dancing around.
* Brevity is the soul of wit. Many of the minimalist philosophies I apply to my life apply to writing as well. Usually, less is more. And generally speaking, things aren't finished when there's nothing left to add, they're finished when there's nothing left you can take away.
* Sometimes a swear word is the correct word.
* Avoid too many "I think," "I  believe," "I feel," clauses. Just say what you think. Get straight to it.
* Don't bury the lede. Capture the main point in a headline or a strong opening sentence.
* Don't fear being mistaken for an LLM. This is something I've dealt with more in recent times. There's a reason why LLM output often includes bullet points, bolded phrases, and em dashes: they really can increase clarity. For a while I wanted to avoid some of those "tells" so no one would mistake the things I wrote for something that fell out of an LLM. No more! Write as effectively as you can, and as humanly as you can.

I'm sure there are other lessons, and I'll update this list as I think of them. But hopefully you'll agree the trend has been a positive one, with every blog post that I write being stronger and better than the one that came before it.
